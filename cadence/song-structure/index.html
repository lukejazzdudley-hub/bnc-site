Verse, chorus and bridge do different work

A verse can move the scene forward: a new detail, event or point of view. A chorus can return to the song’s central thought so that it feels different after each verse. A bridge can change the angle, energy or language before the return. These are useful jobs rather than rules every song must follow.

An intro establishes the world of the song. A pre-chorus can build tension before the chorus. An outro decides what remains when the song ends. Instrumental passages, refrains and rap hooks can take on some of these jobs without using the same labels.

Try a structure before filling every section

One starting sketch is intro → verse → chorus → verse → chorus → bridge → chorus → outro. A shorter rap draft might use hook → verse → hook → verse → hook. Neither sequence specifies a compulsory bar count.

Write a one-sentence purpose beside each section. For a song about missing a train: the first verse shows the missed departure; the chorus names the decision to keep moving; the second verse shows what that decision costs; the bridge questions whether moving is the same as escaping. The final chorus now carries more information even if its words stay the same.

Original arrangement exercise

  1. Choose one idea and write its central thought in ordinary language.
  2. Draft a short hook that can bear repeating. Record it before polishing every word.
  3. Write a verse that gives the hook context. Avoid explaining the whole story twice.
  4. Listen to verse–hook–verse–hook. Mark where attention drops or the return arrives too early.
  5. Try moving, shortening or removing one section. Compare the recordings rather than deciding from the diagram alone.

Keep the arrangement that makes the change in the song audible. A bridge is useful when it earns its place; an empty bridge box is not a reason to write one.

Arrange the song you are actually hearing

In Cadence, keep the lyric, beat and takes with the song, then use Arrange to organise its sections. Continue into the DAW when you need to work on the recorded clips. Watch the real arrangement walkthrough before trying the same steps.

Working to an existing instrumental? Its changes may suggest section boundaries. Write to your type beat and test whether the vocal agrees with those boundaries or creates an intentional contrast. For individual lines, use the guide to syllables, stress and flow.

Common questions

Does every song need a bridge?

No. Add a bridge when a change in perspective or energy helps the song. Test the arrangement by listening; a repeated hook and contrasting verses may already do the work you need.

Can I arrange song sections in Cadence?

Yes. Keep the words, beat and takes with the song, organise sections in Arrange and continue into the mobile DAW to develop the recording.
