From the first idea to a song you can share

Download Cadence for iPhone and iPad or get Cadence for Android. The overall case for Cadence is how much of a song you can develop in the same workspace, wherever you start.

  1. Capture an idea. Start with a line, import a beat or bring in a voice memo and turn the recording into an editable lyric. Use Production Notes to collect sounds from audio or video for later sample export.
  2. Write and revise. See rhyme patterns in the editor, check syllables and explore perfect, slant, multisyllabic, multi-word and assonance matches. Use built-in English or download one of 20 additional language packs.
  3. Rehearse and record. Loop the part you’re working on, record two deliveries and keep the stronger take beside the lyric. Keep the alternate if you want to revisit the performance.
  4. Arrange, mix and export. With Pro, assign takes to timed sections, build the draft in the mobile DAW, use vocal FX and export a mix or aligned WAV stems for a producer.
  5. Return to the work. Organize songs into projects, hear locally available demos in the offline player and use writing activity to see when you’ve been returning to your ideas.

Start with three free notes. The full Draft-to-DAW workflow and export require Pro. Check the current offer in your store.

Cadence offers 21 rhyme language options

Cadence: English is built in, with 20 downloadable rhyme packs, including Spanish, French, Brazilian Portuguese, Hindi, Arabic and Nigerian Pidgin. Reorder the enabled packs to give the language you want priority, then test the suggestions against the way you pronounce the line.

SongRight: Its iPhone and iPad listing documents offline rhymes in English and Spanish. Cadence covers both and adds 19 more rhyme language options. These counts refer to rhyme support.

A real decision you can make before arranging

Imagine a hook that reads well but pushes the last word past the downbeat. Loop the last two bars of the verse and first two of the hook. Record one version with the original line and another that drops two syllables. Keep both takes beside the lyric. If the shorter line gives the hook room to breathe, use that take in the draft and leave the first one available as a reference.

Try the same exercise with the browser syllable counter before opening the app. Then use the section recording walkthrough to see where the take belongs.

Put one of your own songs through the comparison

  1. Bring a short voice memo, four lines you’ve already written or an instrumental you have permission to use. Use the same material and device wherever you’re comparing a shared platform.
  2. Develop one section. Try an alternative rhyme and record a second delivery. Use a loop if your idea has a beat. Close the song, reopen it and find both takes.
  3. Choose the delivery you want to keep. If finishing the arrangement on your phone matters, move the verse and hook into a timeline and test the output you’d actually send.
