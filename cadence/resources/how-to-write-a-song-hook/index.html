How to Write a Song Hook That Fits Your Beat

A hook is the part that catches the ear. It can be the chorus’s strongest line, a melody, or a repeated rhythm. For a lyric hook, start with one thought you want the listener to carry away.

Find the thought worth repeating

Write one sentence of plain speech. For example, “I left the light on because I still thought you would come back.” Shorten it to a line that says enough on its own. “Leave the light on” has a simple stress pattern and leaves the verse room to explain why. The Berklee Online Songwriting Handbook includes a title-first approach to finding a chorus and melody.

Build four bars from one line

Leave the light on
The last bus is gone
Leave the light on
I can’t turn it off

Try one lyric line per bar on a loop. In the first take, begin “Leave” on beat one. In the second, put “Leave the” just before the bar and land “light” on beat one. Keep the same tempo and melody so you can hear the change. If the third line feels predictable, leave a rest after “light” or change the note under “on.”

Hear the same line in two positions

  1. Loop four bars
  2. Move the stressed word
  3. Record both versions

Keep the same melody and tempo while you compare the two deliveries.

Give the hook and chorus different jobs

The hook is the bit you remember. The chorus is the repeated section that can carry it. Here, “Leave the light on” is the hook and the four bars make a short chorus. You could also use the phrase once at the end of each verse, or let an instrumental answer it. Use the song structure guide to decide where the listener should hear it again.

Record it and check what you remember

Record two versions. Step away briefly, then sing back the part you remember before replaying the takes. If the hook disappears, try cutting one syllable or repeating the same rhythm the second time it appears. Compare the versions with the line-by-line syllable counter, then follow the beat-first walkthrough to test the revised line in Cadence.
